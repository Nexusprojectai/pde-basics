Partial Differential Equations

What is a PDE? 🤔

A Partial Differential Equation is an equation that relates a function of several variables to its partial derivatives. It describes how things change in space and time at once.

ut = α uxx

The function u(x,t) depends on position x and time t. Its derivatives tell us how it flows, spreads, vibrates or settles.

💡 Why "partial"? Because we only differentiate with respect to one variable at a time, while treating the others as constant. If you only had one variable, it would be an ODE.

PDE vs ODE

ODEOrdinary — one independent variable. Like tracking a ball: position only depends on time.
y'' + y = 0
PDEPartial — two or more variables. Like tracking heat across a metal rod: depends on position and time.
ut = uxx

If the variables mix together — like x and t inside u — you have a PDE. That's the whole secret. 😄

The Big Three 🏆

Three classic PDEs show up everywhere in physics. Learn these and you've learned 80% of the field.

1 · The Heat Equation 🔥

ut = α ∇²u

Describes how heat spreads over time. In 1D: ut = α uxx. The temperature smooths out — hot spots cool, cold spots warm. α is the thermal diffusivity (how fast heat travels).

2 · The Wave Equation 🌊

utt = c² ∇²u

Describes how vibrations travel — guitar strings, drumheads (you'll like this one 🥁), light, sound. The disturbance moves at speed c without dying out.

3 · Laplace's Equation ⚖️

∇²u = 0

Describes steady states — the final, unchanging picture. Electrostatics, fluid flow, and heat distribution after everything settles. No time variable at all.

Order & Linearity 📏

Order = highest derivative

  • First order: ut + c·ux = 0 advection
  • Second order: utt = c²uxx wave
  • Fourth order: ut = −uxxxx beam bending

Linearity = is u only to the first power?

A PDE is linear if u and all its derivatives appear to the first power, multiplied only by constants (or functions of x,t).

  • ✓ Linear: ut = uxx — clean!
  • ✗ Nonlinear: ut + u·ux = 0 (Burgers' eq — u multiplies u)
  • ✗ Nonlinear: ut = ux² (derivative squared)
💡 Why care? Linear PDEs are friendly: solutions can be added together (superposition). Nonlinear PDEs are the wild chaos — shocks, turbulence, and 1 million-dollar prize problems (Navier–Stokes 👀).

Boundary & Initial Conditions 🚧

A PDE alone has infinitely many solutions. You need extra info to pin down the one that matches your real problem.

Initial Condition (IC) — the starting state

u(x, 0) = f(x)

What the system looks like at time t=0. Like the string's shape the moment you pluck it. 🎸

Boundary Conditions (BC) — the edges

  • Dirichlet: fixed value at the boundary — u(0,t) = 0. Like a clamped string end.
  • Neumann: fixed derivative at the boundary — ux(0,t) = 0. Like an insulated wall (no heat flows out).
  • Robin: a mix — a·u + b·ux = g. Like a wall losing heat to the air.
🔑 Rule of thumb: you need as many conditions as the total order of the equation. 2nd order in space → 2 boundary conditions. 1st order in time → 1 initial condition.

How Do We Solve Them? 🛠️

Step 1 · Separation of Variables (the classic)

Assume the solution splits apart:

u(x,t) = X(x) · T(t)

Plug into the PDE → you get two separate ODEs, one for space, one for time. Solve each → combine → apply conditions. This is how you get Fourier series! 📈

Step 2 · Other tools in the toolbox

  • Fourier transform — convert derivatives into algebra. Great for infinite domains.
  • Green's functions — solve for one point-source, then sum them all up.
  • Numerical methods — when the math is too hard: finite differences, finite elements, spectral methods. This is what computers do! 💻

The 30-second recipe 🍳

  1. Identify the type: heat / wave / Laplace?
  2. Check order & linearity.
  3. Count your IC + BCs — do you have enough?
  4. Try separation of variables → ODEs → solve → superposition.
  5. Apply conditions to nail the constants.
  6. If stuck: go numerical. Computers never judge. 😌
